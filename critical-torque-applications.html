Critical Applications

When Getting
It Wrong Costs
More Than
a Wrench

May 2026Kuna Torque & Tool

In most fastening applications, being 8% off on torque is an inconvenience. In some applications, it's a catastrophe. Understanding where torque accuracy is genuinely critical — and why — is the clearest argument for keeping your calibration current.

These aren't hypothetical risks. Every application below has a documented failure history tied to incorrect torque.

Automotive & Powertrain

Automotive — Engine
Cylinder Head Bolts
Head bolt torque is one of the most specification-critical fastener applications in common shop work. The correct torque — often applied in a specific sequence and sometimes in multiple stages — compresses the head gasket to the precise spec required to seal combustion pressure and coolant passages.

Under-torque: Head gasket failure, coolant in oil, compression loss, engine damage.
Over-torque: Stretched or snapped bolts, warped heads, cracked blocks.

Many head bolts are torque-to-yield (TTY) — designed to stretch slightly during torque, meaning they must be replaced after each use and torqued to exact spec. A wrench that's 10% off on a TTY bolt application can mean a head gasket job becomes an engine replacement.
Automotive — Wheels
Wheel Lug Nuts
Lug nut torque specs exist for a reason: too loose and the wheel separates from the vehicle; too tight and you warp rotors, stretch studs, and make lug nuts impossible to remove in a roadside emergency — or worse, crack the wheel hub.

This is one of the most common applications where impact guns are used without torque verification, and one of the most common sources of torque wrench calibration failure reports we see. A miscalibrated wrench used on lug nuts is a direct safety issue for anyone in that vehicle.

Aviation & Aerospace

Aviation — Rotorcraft
The Jesus Nut
The main rotor retaining nut on certain helicopter designs — colloquially known as the Jesus nut — is the single fastener that connects the main rotor to the mast. If it comes loose, the rotor separates from the aircraft.

The name is blunt: if that nut fails, the only thing left to do is pray. It's used here not for shock value but because it's the clearest possible illustration of what a critical fastener means — a single torque-specification fastener whose failure is unrecoverable.

Aviation maintenance documentation requirements for torque are extensive precisely because of applications like this. Calibrated wrenches with documented traceability aren't optional in certified maintenance environments — they're regulatory requirements.

Heavy Truck & Fleet

Fleet — Drivetrain
Axle & Hub Fasteners
Wheel end fasteners on commercial trucks carry enormous loads under constant vibration. Under-torqued wheel bearings allow preload loss and accelerated wear. Over-torqued bearings overheat and fail.

A wheel-off event on a commercial truck at highway speed is among the most dangerous failure modes in ground transportation. DOT regulations and fleet maintenance standards require documented, traceable torque procedures specifically because of the consequences of getting it wrong.

Structural & Industrial

Structural — Construction
High-Strength Structural Bolting
ASTM A325 and A490 high-strength bolts used in structural steel connections require verified, documented torque procedures. The AISC and RCSC specifications for structural joints prescribe specific installation methods — including torque requirements that must be verified with calibrated equipment.

Structural connections in buildings, bridges, and industrial equipment carry static and dynamic loads over decades. A bolt installed at 70% of specified torque that loosens over time under thermal cycling and vibration can initiate a progressive connection failure. Structural bolt installation is one of the few non-aviation applications with explicit regulatory requirements for calibrated torque tools.

The Common Thread

Every critical torque application shares the same characteristic: the consequences of being wrong are disproportionately larger than the cost of being right.

Getting head bolt torque right costs the price of a calibration. Getting it wrong costs an engine. Getting lug nut torque right costs the price of a calibration. Getting it wrong costs a lawsuit. Getting structural bolt torque right costs the price of a calibration. Getting it wrong costs lives.

// The Bottom Line

The applications above are not edge cases. Cylinder heads, wheel fasteners, and heavy equipment are the bread and butter of shop and fleet work across the Treasure Valley. If your shop does this work, you need calibrated tools — and you need to be able to document it.
